Rescue Vehicles In, but Patient Protection Remains Unenforced

Commentary welcomes Davao del Sur’s new rescue vehicles while calling for stronger enforcement of emergency-care protections, transparent medicine stocks, and accessible hospital complaint channels.

October 20, 2026 at 8:46 AM· Davao del Sur
By Community Affairs Desk Davao del Sur · October 20, 2026 A WELCOME INVESTMENT TWENTY brand-new rescue vehicles rolled out to barangays across eight municipalities of Davao del Sur this week—a ₱17.7 million investment in faster emergency response and community disaster readiness. Each unit, valued at ₱885,542, was turned over by Governor Yvonne R. Cagas during ceremonies at the Capitol compound, complete with orientations on insurance, proper use, and maintenance. It is, by all accounts, a commendable step forward. Communities in Bansalan, Hagonoy, Padada, Sulop, Kiblawan, Malalag, Magsaysay, and Santa Cruz are better equipped to respond to emergencies. Lives will likely be saved because help can arrive sooner. A GROWING DISCONNECT YET as the vehicles were being deployed, a persistent gap remains unaddressed: the assurance that patients once they reach public hospitals will actually receive the care they are legally entitled to—without deposits, without hidden costs, and with essential medicines provided free of charge. Families continue to report a frustrating reality: they can be rushed faster to hospital, only to face financial barriers the moment they arrive. Republic Act No. 10932 explicitly prohibits hospitals—public and private—from demanding advance deposits or down payments before administering emergency care. PhilHealth’s No-Balance Billing Policy mandates that eligible patients should not be charged for services covered by their benefits. Yet on the ground, enforcement appears uneven at best. Complaints reaching community monitors remain consistent: patients are still directed to purchase medicines outside even when supplies are reportedly available in hospital pharmacies; additional fees surface at discharge despite “no payment required” assurances; and when concerns are raised, some are told simply to “go to a private hospital instead.” THE TRANSPORT-CARE PARADOX THE paradox is clear: we have strengthened our capacity to bring people to the hospital, but not necessarily our commitment to care for them fairly once they are there. A rescue vehicle fulfills its purpose only if the destination honors the same urgency to protect lives—including from financial ruin. “Puhunan sa pagdala — maayo. Puhunan sa pagtambal — kinahanglan usab masiguro.” Fast response means little if families hesitate to call for help because they fear what awaits them at the admitting section. Emergency readiness cannot end at the hospital gate. It must extend through admission, treatment, and discharge—free from cost-shifting, illegal drug procurement demands, and dismissive responses to legitimate concerns. WHAT REMAINS TO BE DONE THE provincial government’s investment in emergency response deserves recognition. But it should also prompt a parallel commitment from health authorities. Three straightforward measures could begin to close the gap: - First, DOH Region XI and hospital administrators should publicly confirm that zero-billing policies are being strictly enforced at the Davao del Sur Provincial Hospital and all district facilities. - Second, essential medicine stock levels should be made accessible information—not hidden from public view—so families know what should be available. - Third, a visible, accessible complaint mechanism should be operational at every public hospital entrance, so concerns are addressed before they become grievances. Rescue vehicles bring people to safety. Public health laws must keep them safe from harm—including financial harm—once they arrive. These are not competing priorities. They are two sides of the same commitment: that in Davao del Sur, every life is protected—from the moment help is called, to the moment recovery is complete.
